\section{Operational Background}
\label{sec:operational-background}

\subsection{Changes in Airspace and Facility Availability}

The FAA concept of operations allows UAM corridors to open and close and requires operators to monitor weather and wind conditions before and during flight. In a simulated vertiport closure, two airborne flights were diverted, and one flight awaiting departure was canceled.~\cite{FAA2023_L088, Prince2025_L041}. 

\observation{B1}{Loss of availability may occur after affected aircraft have departed. Therefore, the response must account for their current flight states and the remaining available options.}

\subsection{Normal Operations and Disruption Response}

A network design with reserve capacity adds backup vertiports to the vertiports that provide regular service~\cite{Wei2024_L063}. Under a fixed infrastructure budget, the backup vertiports are placed where they serve the most demand, and under some network topologies this leaves part of the network with little reserve support~\cite{Wei2024_L063}. The authors suggest examining the tradeoff between operational efficiency and a more even distribution of contingency support~\cite{Wei2024_L063}. Closure verification also accounts for diverted flights and continuing traffic that share landing capacity~\cite{Wei2024_L069}.

\observation{B2}{The same network design supports both normal service and disruption response. Therefore, design comparisons should assess both functions under their respective operating conditions.}

\section{Literature Review and Observations}
\label{sec:literature-review}

\subsection{Operating Context and Traffic-Management Frameworks}
\label{sec:literature-1}

Operating concepts and airspace-design studies for urban air mobility (UAM) relate airspace organization to aircraft capabilities, operating procedures, and coordination responsibilities~\cite{FAA2023_L088,Bauranov2021_L024}. Traffic-management studies examine how functions such as access control, flight planning, and conflict management interact and affect shared operations~\cite{Ramee2021_L028,Prince2025_L041}.

\observation{1}{When a localized portion of the airspace becomes unavailable, affected flights continue to share operating rules and resources with surrounding traffic. The response is therefore a
traffic-coordination problem as well as a routing problem.}

\subsection{Airspace Geometry, Aircraft Performance, and Energy}
\label{sec:literature-2}

A graph abstraction of urban airspace shows how its connectivity changes with the required passage width and with altitude~\cite{Cho2021_L037}. Corridor-protection and contingency-planning studies relate the space needed for maneuvering to the motion limits of the aircraft~\cite{Choi2026_L036,Ortlieb2021_L020}. Contingency landing management assesses reachability using the current aircraft state, performance model, and estimated battery end-of-discharge time~\cite{Kim2026_L066}. Emergency-trajectory planning accounts for corridor limits, the protected region around an emergency aircraft, and aircraft turning constraints~\cite{Variny2025_L015}.

\observation{2}{The space and energy needed for a response depend on the aircraft state and performance limits. Therefore, candidate routes through the remaining airspace need to be checked for these requirements and for separation from surrounding traffic.}

\subsection{Traffic Planning and Coordination}
\label{sec:literature-3}

Strategic and pre-tactical methods manage planned interactions through flight-level assignment~\cite{Tang2021_L013} or through the timing of waypoint occupancy~\cite{Pang2024_L017}. Tactical and hybrid approaches handle conflicts through different combinations of preflight planning and in-flight coordination~\cite{Veytia2022_L051}. In simulations with communication latency and loss, separation performance was slightly lower than in earlier results with perfect communication and differed between centralized and decentralized control~\cite{Yang2020_L053}.

\observation{3}{Traffic-planning and coordination methods differ in their decision timing, information requirements, and permitted changes to flight level, timing, or trajectory. Their use after a blockage depends on whether these requirements and permitted actions match the information and options available for aircraft already airborne.}

\subsection{Disruption Response and Contingency Management}
\label{sec:literature-4}

Adaptive replanning changes flight paths and timing in response to disturbances in the operating environment~\cite{Wu2021_L012}. Contingency landing management assesses whether a degraded aircraft can still reach a landing site~\cite{Kim2026_L066}. Emergency-trajectory studies plan lower-priority flights around priority traffic~\cite{Variny2025_L015,PintoNeto2025_L079}, and one of them examines how the resulting delays propagate and can be reduced~\cite{Variny2025_L015}. These methods address different initiating events and pursue different response objectives~\cite{Wu2021_L012,Kim2026_L066,PintoNeto2025_L079}.

\observation{4}{The outcome used to define a completed response depends on the event and the purpose of the procedure. Resolving an immediate conflict, completing an emergency landing, and rejoining a route to continue a flight require different completion criteria.}

\subsection{Network Configuration and Resilience}
\label{sec:literature-5}

UAM network design connects facility locations, fleet resources, and operating decisions to the service the network provides~\cite{Wang2025_L087}. Backup vertiports and routes can increase expected throughput during disruptions, but the gains differ across pairs of origins and destinations~\cite{Wei2024_L063}. Closure verification accounts for diverted flights that share backup landing capacity with flights from other routes and with continuing traffic~\cite{Wei2024_L069}. Simulation-based replanning also checks subsequent flights that depend on the same aircraft or vertiport resources~\cite{Prince2025_L041}.

\observation{5}{Flights from different origins and destinations may depend on the same backup vertiports and connecting routes. Their combined demand on these resources needs to be considered when evaluating redundancy during a disruption.}

\subsection{Performance Evaluation, Uncertainty, and Verification}
\label{sec:literature-6}

A simulation evaluation reports throughput, delay, and separation-related outcomes~\cite{Prince2025_L041}, and a level-of-service method assesses passenger, air taxi operator, and vertiport operator performance with separate metrics~\cite{Schweiger2023_L090}. Verification studies establish properties such as landing-capacity compliance or the feasibility of contingency trajectories, but only under the assumptions they specify~\cite{Kim2026_L066,Wei2024_L069}. A probabilistic airspace model relates the required protected space to the uncertainty representation and the selected risk level~\cite{Hu2026_L085}.

\observation{6}{Feasibility, safety-related properties, and service performance are different kinds of claims and call for different evidence. An evaluation needs to distinguish these claims and state the aircraft and network effects measured and the assumptions under which its conclusions hold.}

\subsection{Disruption Recovery and Joint Design in Related Transportation Systems}
\label{sec:literature-7}

Disruption studies in railway and airline networks relate infrastructure availability and resource reassignment to service continuity and subsequent delays~\cite{Trommelen2022_L054,Zhu2019_L060,Suh2017_L004}. A railway corridor study evaluated track layout and dispatching strategy together to quantify their combined effect on corridor operations~\cite{Tosstorff2026_L046}. In UAM, the effect of an airspace structure was found to depend on the traffic-management approach~\cite{Ramee2021_L028}, and different separation-management concepts distributed traffic differently over the same airspace~\cite{Veytia2022_L051}.

\observation{7}{The effect of a spatial design change depends on how traffic uses the available airspace under the disruption conditions. Therefore, spatial changes and operating procedures need to be evaluated together when improving a corridor design.}

\section{Research Formulation}
\label{sec:research-formulation}

\subsection{Study Context}
\label{sec:study-context}

This study addresses aircraft rerouting and surrounding-traffic coordination when a localized portion of a Cooperative Area (CA) becomes unavailable at a fixed location. A CA is an airspace volume, such as a UAM corridor, within which operations are cooperatively managed~\cite{FAA2023_L088}. The response takes place within the remaining available airspace and ends for each rerouted aircraft when it rejoins an available route for continued flight.

\subsection{Research Gap and Study Focus}
\label{sec:research-gap}

Existing studies provide methods for trajectory planning, contingency management, closure verification, and network design under their respective operating assumptions~\cite{Variny2025_L015,Kim2026_L066,Wei2024_L069,Wei2024_L063}. For the localized blockage considered here, the research need is to connect aircraft-level response feasibility with corridor-level evaluation and design improvement. This connection requires consistent treatment of aircraft state and energy, surrounding traffic, shared resources, and performance during normal operations. The gap to be examined is whether existing methods support this connection under a common set of operating assumptions. A targeted comparison will distinguish capabilities that can be reused from those that require further development.

\subsection{Research Objective}
\label{sec:research-objective}

\begin{quote}
\textit{This study aims to develop an integrated framework for emergency procedure development, performance evaluation, and corridor design improvement under localized blockages within a Cooperative Area. The framework will assess emergency response together with the efficiency of normal operations.}
\end{quote}

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

\subsubsection{RQ1. Emergency Procedure Development}
\label{sec:rq1}

\begin{quote}
\textit{A localized portion of a Cooperative Area at a fixed location becomes unavailable for passage. How can emergency procedures be designed to reroute and coordinate affected aircraft within the remaining available airspace while satisfying separation, aircraft performance, and energy constraints?}
\end{quote}

The work under RQ1 will develop emergency procedures and demonstrate safe and feasible flight for every aircraft represented in a defined case study. The assessment will include each aircraft's current flight state and remaining energy, together with surrounding traffic subject to coordination or separation requirements. Efficiency will not be an optimization objective in RQ1. Any conclusion about feasibility will apply only to the conditions and assumptions examined.

\subsubsection{RQ2. Performance Evaluation}
\label{sec:rq2}

\begin{quote}
\textit{How can emergency procedures and corridor designs be evaluated to assess their effects on aircraft and network performance under localized blockages?}
\end{quote}

The work under RQ2 will establish an evaluation approach that connects procedure and corridor design choices to aircraft and network outcomes. It will define the operating conditions, comparison cases, evaluation period, and metrics needed to interpret those outcomes. The evaluation will assess constraint satisfaction, response completion, and effects on surrounding traffic. Normal operations will provide a reference for design comparisons.

\subsubsection{RQ3. Corridor Design Improvement}
\label{sec:rq3}

\begin{quote}
\textit{How can spatial corridor design and operational design be jointly optimized to improve emergency response while maintaining normal operating efficiency within a Cooperative Area?}
\end{quote}

The work under RQ3 will use the evaluation from RQ2 to examine spatial arrangements and operating rules together. Candidate designs will be compared for both normal operations and localized blockages. Emergency response improvements will be assessed alongside any changes in normal operating efficiency. Each candidate must satisfy the separation, aircraft performance, and energy requirements used in procedure development. The criteria for acceptable normal performance will be defined before optimization.
