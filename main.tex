\section{Operational Background}
\label{sec:operational-background}

\subsection{Changes in Airspace and Facility Availability}

The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) concept of operations allows Urban Air Mobility (UAM) Corridors to be opened and closed~\cite{FAA2023_L088}. The concept also states that operators monitor weather and winds before and during flight~\cite{FAA2023_L088}. In a simulated vertiport closure, two airborne flights were diverted and one flight awaiting departure was canceled~\cite{Prince2025_L041}.

\observation{B1}{A loss of availability can occur after the affected aircraft have departed. Therefore, the response needs to account for their current flight states and the options that remain available.}

\subsection{Normal Operations and Disruption Response}

A network design with reserve capacity adds backup vertiports to those that provide regular service~\cite{Wei2024_L063}. The design method maximizes expected network throughput under a fixed infrastructure budget. Thus, backup vertiports are placed at sites that are likely to serve more demand. As a result, some origin-destination pairs can receive limited reserve support in certain network topologies. The tradeoff between operational efficiency and more even reserve coverage across origin-destination pairs is left for future study~\cite{Wei2024_L063}. Closure verification also accounts for diverted flights and continuing traffic that share landing capacity~\cite{Wei2024_L069}.

\observation{B2}{The same network design supports both normal service and disruption response. Therefore, design comparisons should assess both functions under their respective operating conditions.}

\section{Literature Review and Observations}
\label{sec:literature-review}

\subsection{Operating Context and Traffic-Management Frameworks}
\label{sec:literature-1}

Operating concepts and airspace design studies for UAM relate airspace organization to aircraft capabilities, operating procedures, and coordination responsibilities~\cite{FAA2023_L088,Bauranov2021_L024}. Traffic management studies examine how access control, flight planning, and conflict management interact and how they affect shared operations~\cite{Ramee2021_L028,Prince2025_L041}.

\observation{1}{When a localized portion of the airspace becomes unavailable, affected flights continue to share operating rules and resources with surrounding traffic. The response is therefore a traffic-coordination problem as well as a routing problem.}

\subsection{Airspace Geometry, Aircraft Performance, and Energy}
\label{sec:literature-2}

A graph abstraction of urban airspace shows how connectivity changes with the required passage width and with altitude~\cite{Cho2021_L037}. Studies of corridor protection and contingency planning relate the space needed for maneuvering to the motion limits of the aircraft~\cite{Choi2026_L036,Ortlieb2021_L020}. Contingency landing management evaluates reachability based on the current aircraft state, performance model, and estimated battery end-of-discharge time~\cite{Kim2026_L066}. Emergency trajectory planning considers corridor limits, the protected region around an emergency aircraft, and aircraft turning constraints~\cite{Variny2025_L015}.

\observation{2}{The space and energy needed for a response depend on the aircraft state and performance limits. Therefore, candidate routes through the remaining airspace need to be checked for these requirements and for separation from surrounding traffic.}

\subsection{Traffic Planning and Coordination}
\label{sec:literature-3}

Strategic and pre-tactical methods manage planned interactions through flight-level assignment or through the timing of waypoint occupancy~\cite{Tang2021_L013,Pang2024_L017}. Tactical and hybrid approaches handle conflicts through different combinations of preflight planning and in-flight coordination~\cite{Veytia2022_L051}. Separation performance in simulations with communication latency and loss was slightly lower than in earlier results with perfect communication~\cite{Yang2020_L053}. The performance also differed between centralized and decentralized control~\cite{Yang2020_L053}.

\observation{3}{Traffic-planning and coordination methods differ in their decision timing, information requirements, and permitted changes to flight level, timing, or trajectory. Therefore, a method can be used after a blockage only if these requirements and permitted actions match the information and options available to aircraft that are already airborne.}

\subsection{Disruption Response and Contingency Management}
\label{sec:literature-4}

Adaptive replanning changes flight paths and timing in response to disturbances in the operating environment~\cite{Wu2021_L012}. In-flight replanning with a battery model steers an aircraft around a newly detected obstacle and returns it to its original course~\cite{Kim2024_L042}. Contingency landing management assesses whether a degraded aircraft can still reach a landing site~\cite{Kim2026_L066}. Emergency trajectory studies plan lower-priority flights around priority traffic~\cite{Variny2025_L015,PintoNeto2025_L079}. One of these studies also examines how the resulting delays propagate and how they can be reduced~\cite{Variny2025_L015}. These methods address different initiating events and pursue different response objectives~\cite{Wu2021_L012,Kim2026_L066,PintoNeto2025_L079}.

\observation{4}{The outcome used to define a completed response depends on the event and the purpose of the procedure. Therefore, a response in which the aircraft rejoins a route and continues to its destination needs completion criteria that extend to landing. These criteria differ from those for resolving an immediate conflict or completing an emergency landing.}

\subsection{Network Configuration and Resilience}
\label{sec:literature-5}

UAM network design links facility locations, fleet resources, and operating decisions to the services provided by the network~\cite{Wang2025_L087}. Backup vertiports and routes can increase expected throughput during disruptions, but the gains differ across origin-destination pairs~\cite{Wei2024_L063}. Closure verification considers diverted flights that share backup landing capacity with flights from other routes and with continuing traffic~\cite{Wei2024_L069}. Simulation-based replanning also evaluates subsequent flights that rely on the same aircraft or vertiport resources~\cite{Prince2025_L041}.

\observation{5}{Flights from different origins and destinations may depend on the same backup vertiports and connecting routes. Their combined demand on these resources needs to be considered when evaluating redundancy during a disruption.}

\subsection{Performance Evaluation, Uncertainty, and Verification}
\label{sec:literature-6}

A simulation evaluation reports throughput, delay, and separation-related outcomes~\cite{Prince2025_L041}. A level-of-service method assesses performance with separate metrics for the passenger, the air taxi operator, and the vertiport operator~\cite{Schweiger2023_L090}. Verification studies establish properties such as landing-capacity compliance or the feasibility of contingency trajectories, but only under specified assumptions~\cite{Wei2024_L069,Kim2026_L066}. A probabilistic airspace model relates the required protected space to the uncertainty representation and the chosen risk level~\cite{Hu2026_L085}.

\observation{6}{Feasibility, safety-related properties, and service performance are different kinds of claims and call for different evidence. An evaluation needs to distinguish these claims and state the aircraft and network effects measured and the assumptions under which its conclusions hold.}

\subsection{Disruption Recovery and Joint Design in Related Transportation Systems}
\label{sec:literature-7}

Disruption studies in railway and airline networks connect infrastructure availability and resource reassignment to service continuity and subsequent delays~\cite{Trommelen2022_L054,Zhu2019_L060,Suh2017_L004}. One railway corridor study evaluated track layout and dispatching strategy jointly to quantify their combined effect on corridor operations~\cite{Tosstorff2026_L046}. The effect of an airspace structure in UAM depends on the traffic management approach~\cite{Ramee2021_L028}. Different separation management concepts also distribute traffic differently over the same airspace~\cite{Veytia2022_L051}.

\observation{7}{The effect of a spatial design change depends on how traffic uses the available airspace under the disruption conditions. Therefore, spatial changes and operating procedures need to be evaluated together when improving a corridor design.}

\section{Research Formulation}
\label{sec:research-formulation}

\subsection{Study Context}
\label{sec:study-context}

This study examines how aircraft are rerouted and coordinated with surrounding traffic when a localized portion of a Cooperative Area (CA) becomes unavailable at a fixed location. A CA is defined as an airspace volume, such as a UAM Corridor, within which cooperatively managed operations can occur~\cite{FAA2023_L088}. The blockage occurs after flights have started, and it remains in place for the rest of the period studied. Affected aircraft that are airborne are rerouted within the remaining available airspace. Affected flights that have not departed are held on the ground. Route rejoining is treated as an intermediate event. The response of an aircraft is complete only when that aircraft has landed.

\subsection{Research Gap and Study Focus}
\label{sec:research-gap}

The observations in Sections~\ref{sec:operational-background} and~\ref{sec:literature-review} lead to three requirements for the localized blockage considered in this study. First, the response applies to aircraft that are already airborne, and it involves both rerouting and coordination with surrounding traffic (Observations B1, 1, and 3). Each candidate route needs to be checked against the state, performance limits, and remaining energy of the aircraft (Observation 2). The response is complete only when the aircraft has rejoined a route, continued its flight, and landed (Observation 4). Second, an evaluation of the response needs to separate feasibility, safety-related properties, and service performance, and to state the aircraft and network effects that are measured (Observation 6). The evaluation also needs to include the traffic that shares the same resources (Observation 5). Third, spatial changes and operating procedures need to be evaluated together, under both normal operations and the blockage (Observations B2 and 7).

Existing studies address parts of these requirements under their respective operating assumptions. In-flight replanning with a battery model has been demonstrated in free route airspace for a single aircraft that avoids a newly detected obstacle and then returns to its original course~\cite{Kim2024_L042}. The same planner adjusts the trajectories of three arriving aircraft when a diverted aircraft joins their landing sequence~\cite{Kim2024_L042}. Rerouting within a flight corridor has been demonstrated with turning limits, but without an energy model, for aircraft on approach that give way to an emergency aircraft~\cite{Variny2025_L015}. A simulation evaluation in cooperative airspace quantifies separation and delay outcomes for route structure and scheduling alternatives under normal operations~\cite{Prince2025_L041}. For a vertiport closure and a route change, the same evaluation reports the sequence of events and participant observations~\cite{Prince2025_L041}. Reserve capacity has been optimized at the network level for expected throughput during disruptions~\cite{Wei2024_L063}. Spatial layout and operating strategy have been evaluated together for railway corridors and, under normal operations, for UAM airspace structures~\cite{Tosstorff2026_L046,Ramee2021_L028}.

These observations lead to the following gaps.

\gap{1}{The studies reviewed do not provide an emergency procedure for a localized blockage within a CA. Such a procedure needs to reroute several airborne aircraft through the remaining airspace and coordinate them with surrounding traffic until they have rejoined an available route and landed. Separation, aircraft performance, and energy constraints need to be satisfied for each aircraft until landing.}

\gap{2}{In the studies reviewed, the effect of a localized blockage within a CA on aircraft and network outcomes has not been quantified for different emergency procedures and network designs. Thus, the factors with the largest effect and the interactions between spatial and procedural factors have not been identified.}

\gap{3}{The studies reviewed do not provide a design method that improves the network design and the operating procedures together for the response to a localized blockage. Such a method also needs to maintain normal operating efficiency.}

The gaps are stated for the studies reviewed here. A targeted comparison with the closest studies will be used to distinguish the capabilities that can be reused from those that require further development. Gap 1, Gap 2, and Gap 3 will be addressed by RQ1, RQ2, and RQ3 in Section~\ref{sec:research-questions}. RQ1 will explore whether a feasible response exists for different blockage locations. RQ2 will measure the response under common conditions and metrics, and RQ3 will use these measurements to improve the design. Each candidate design will then be assessed again with the same procedure and evaluation. This sequence requires consistent treatment of aircraft state and energy, surrounding traffic, shared resources, and performance during normal operations.

\subsection{Research Objective}
\label{sec:research-objective}

\begin{quote}
\textit{This study aims to develop an integrated framework for emergency procedure development, performance evaluation, and network design improvement under localized blockages within a Cooperative Area. The framework will evaluate emergency response together with the efficiency of normal operations.}
\end{quote}

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

\subsubsection{RQ1. Emergency Procedure Development}
\label{sec:rq1}

\begin{quote}
\textit{A localized portion of a Cooperative Area at a fixed location becomes unavailable for passage. How can emergency procedures be designed to reroute and coordinate affected aircraft within the remaining available airspace while satisfying separation, aircraft performance, and energy constraints?}
\end{quote}

Emergency procedures will be developed under RQ1, and the conditions under which they give a feasible response will be explored. Each simulation run will start with all aircraft on the ground and will follow a flight schedule. The blockage will be introduced after flights have started and will remain in place until the end of the run. Affected aircraft that are airborne will be rerouted, and affected flights that have not departed will be held on the ground. A run will end only when every flight that departs has landed, because an aircraft is considered safe only after landing. Flight schedules and blockage locations will be sampled across Monte Carlo replications. Separation, aircraft performance, and energy constraints will be checked for each aircraft until landing. These checks will include surrounding traffic. A blockage location for which no feasible response is found will be reported as such. Efficiency will not be an optimization objective in RQ1. Any conclusion about feasibility will apply only to the conditions and assumptions examined.

\hypothesis{1}{The emergency procedure will combine three elements. It will start from the state of each affected aircraft at blockage onset, check each candidate route against separation, aircraft performance, and energy constraints until landing, and coordinate the affected aircraft with surrounding traffic. A response produced with these elements is expected to bring every affected aircraft to landing without a constraint violation. This outcome will be checked for each sampled flight schedule and blockage location. A case for which no feasible response is found will be recorded together with the constraint that prevents it. For the same samples, a procedure without the energy check or without the coordination step is expected to produce more constraint violations or more incomplete runs.}

\subsubsection{RQ2. Performance Evaluation}
\label{sec:rq2}

\begin{quote}
\textit{How does a localized blockage within a Cooperative Area affect aircraft and network performance, and how do the emergency procedure and the network design change this effect?}
\end{quote}

An evaluation approach will be established under RQ2 to connect procedure and network design choices to aircraft and network outcomes. A parametric study will then be performed over blockage conditions, traffic conditions, procedure parameters, and network design parameters. The study will identify the factors that have the largest effect on these outcomes and the interactions between spatial and procedural factors. Normal operations will provide a reference for design comparisons.

\hypothesis{2}{The effect of a localized blockage on aircraft and network outcomes depends on the combination of network design parameters and procedure parameters. Thus, the effect of a spatial change differs between procedures. A change that improves the emergency response is also expected to reduce normal operating efficiency over part of the design range.}

\subsubsection{RQ3. Network Design Improvement}
\label{sec:rq3}

\begin{quote}
\textit{How can the spatial design of the network, from vertiport locations to corridor geometry, and the operating procedures be jointly optimized to improve emergency response while maintaining normal operating efficiency?}
\end{quote}

The evaluation approach from RQ2 will be used under RQ3 to examine the spatial design of the network and the operating rules together. The spatial design ranges from vertiport locations to corridor geometry. Candidate designs will be compared for both normal operations and localized blockages. Improvements in emergency response will be assessed together with any changes in normal operating efficiency. Each candidate must satisfy the separation, aircraft performance, and energy requirements used in procedure development. The criteria for acceptable normal performance will be defined before optimization.

\hypothesis{3}{For the same requirement on normal operating efficiency, selecting the network design and the operating procedures together will give a better emergency response than selecting them one after the other.}

\subsection{Overarching Hypothesis}
\label{sec:overarching-hypothesis}

The three working hypotheses are combined in the following overarching hypothesis.

\begin{quote}
\textit{The emergency response to a localized blockage within a Cooperative Area can be improved while normal operating efficiency is maintained if a framework performs three tasks together. The framework generates a feasible emergency procedure for a given network design, quantifies the effect of the blockage for different procedures and network designs, and selects the network design and the operating procedures together.}
\end{quote}
