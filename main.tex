\section{Operational Background}
\label{sec:operational-background}

\subsection{Changes in Airspace and Facility Availability}

The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) concept of operations allows for the opening and closing of urban air mobility (UAM) corridors and states that operators monitor weather and wind conditions both before and during flight~\cite{FAA2023_L088}. In a simulated vertiport closure, two airborne flights were diverted, and one flight awaiting departure was canceled~\cite{Prince2025_L041}.

\observation{B1}{Loss of availability may occur after affected aircraft have departed. Therefore, the response must account for their current flight states and the remaining available options.}

\subsection{Normal Operations and Disruption Response}

A network design that incorporates reserve capacity adds backup vertiports in addition to those providing regular service~\cite{Wei2024_L063}. When infrastructure budgets are fixed, maximizing expected network throughput prioritizes sites that are likely to serve higher demand. In certain network topologies, this approach may result in some origin-destination pairs receiving limited reserve support. The authors recommend evaluating the tradeoff between operational efficiency and more even reserve coverage across origin-destination pairs~\cite{Wei2024_L063}. Closure verification further accounts for diverted flights and ongoing traffic that utilize shared landing capacity~\cite{Wei2024_L069}.

\observation{B2}{The same network design supports both normal service and disruption response. Therefore, design comparisons should assess both functions under their respective operating conditions.}

\section{Literature Review and Observations}
\label{sec:literature-review}

\subsection{Operating Context and Traffic-Management Frameworks}
\label{sec:literature-1}

Studies on operating concepts and airspace design for UAM establish relationships between airspace organization, aircraft capabilities, operating procedures, and coordination responsibilities~\cite{FAA2023_L088,Bauranov2021_L024}. Research on traffic management examines the interactions among access control, flight planning, and conflict management, and their collective influence on shared operations~\cite{Ramee2021_L028,Prince2025_L041}.

\observation{1}{When a localized portion of the airspace becomes unavailable, affected flights continue to share operating rules and resources with surrounding traffic. The response is therefore a traffic-coordination problem as well as a routing problem.}

\subsection{Airspace Geometry, Aircraft Performance, and Energy}
\label{sec:literature-2}

A graph-based abstraction of urban airspace illustrates how connectivity varies with required passage width and altitude~\cite{Cho2021_L037}. Corridor protection and contingency planning studies relate the maneuvering space required to the motion limits of the aircraft~\cite{Choi2026_L036,Ortlieb2021_L020}. Contingency landing management evaluates reachability based on the current aircraft state, performance model, and estimated battery end-of-discharge time~\cite{Kim2026_L066}. Emergency trajectory planning considers corridor limits, the protected region around an emergency aircraft, and aircraft turning constraints~\cite{Variny2025_L015}.

\observation{2}{The space and energy needed for a response depend on the aircraft state and performance limits. Therefore, candidate routes through the remaining airspace need to be checked for these requirements and for separation from surrounding traffic.}

\subsection{Traffic Planning and Coordination}
\label{sec:literature-3}

Strategic and pre-tactical methods manage planned interactions through flight-level assignment or waypoint occupancy timing~\cite{Tang2021_L013,Pang2024_L017}. Tactical and hybrid approaches address conflicts using various combinations of preflight planning and in-flight coordination~\cite{Veytia2022_L051}. Simulations involving communication latency and loss demonstrated slightly reduced separation performance compared to earlier results with perfect communication, and performance varied between centralized and decentralized control~\cite{Yang2020_L053}.

\observation{3}{Traffic-planning and coordination methods differ in their decision timing, information requirements, and permitted changes to flight level, timing, or trajectory. Their use after a blockage depends on whether these requirements and permitted actions match the information and options available for aircraft already airborne.}

\subsection{Disruption Response and Contingency Management}
\label{sec:literature-4}

Adaptive replanning modifies flight paths and timing in response to disturbances in the operating environment~\cite{Wu2021_L012}. Contingency landing management determines whether a degraded aircraft can reach a landing site~\cite{Kim2026_L066}. Emergency trajectory studies plan lower-priority flights around priority traffic~\cite{Variny2025_L015,PintoNeto2025_L079}, with one study examining how the resulting delays propagate and can be mitigated~\cite{Variny2025_L015}. These methods address distinct initiating events and pursue different response objectives~\cite{Wu2021_L012,Kim2026_L066,PintoNeto2025_L079}.

\observation{4}{The outcome used to define a completed response depends on the event and the purpose of the procedure. Resolving an immediate conflict, completing an emergency landing, and rejoining a route to continue a flight require different completion criteria.}

\subsection{Network Configuration and Resilience}
\label{sec:literature-5}

UAM network design links facility locations, fleet resources, and operating decisions to the services provided by the network~\cite{Wang2025_L087}. Backup vertiports and routes can enhance expected throughput during disruptions, although the benefits vary across different origin-destination pairs~\cite{Wei2024_L063}. Closure verification considers diverted flights that share backup landing capacity with flights from other routes and with ongoing traffic~\cite{Wei2024_L069}. Simulation-based replanning also evaluates subsequent flights that rely on the same aircraft or vertiport resources~\cite{Prince2025_L041}.

\observation{5}{Flights from different origins and destinations may depend on the same backup vertiports and connecting routes. Their combined demand on these resources needs to be considered when evaluating redundancy during a disruption.}

\subsection{Performance Evaluation, Uncertainty, and Verification}
\label{sec:literature-6}

Simulation evaluations report throughput, delay, and separation-related outcomes~\cite{Prince2025_L041}, while level-of-service methods assess passenger, air taxi operator, and vertiport operator performance using distinct metrics~\cite{Schweiger2023_L090}. Verification studies establish properties such as landing-capacity compliance or the feasibility of contingency trajectories, but only under specified assumptions~\cite{Wei2024_L069,Kim2026_L066}. A probabilistic airspace model relates the required protected space to the uncertainty representation and the chosen risk level~\cite{Hu2026_L085}.

\observation{6}{Feasibility, safety-related properties, and service performance are different kinds of claims and call for different evidence. An evaluation needs to distinguish these claims and state the aircraft and network effects measured and the assumptions under which its conclusions hold.}

\subsection{Disruption Recovery and Joint Design in Related Transportation Systems}
\label{sec:literature-7}

Disruption studies in railway and airline networks connect infrastructure availability and resource reassignment to service continuity and subsequent delays~\cite{Trommelen2022_L054,Zhu2019_L060,Suh2017_L004}. One railway corridor study evaluated track layout and dispatching strategy jointly to quantify their combined effect on corridor operations~\cite{Tosstorff2026_L046}. In UAM, the impact of airspace structure depends on the traffic management approach~\cite{Ramee2021_L028}, and different separation management concepts distribute traffic differently over the same airspace~\cite{Veytia2022_L051}.

\observation{7}{The effect of a spatial design change depends on how traffic uses the available airspace under the disruption conditions. Therefore, spatial changes and operating procedures need to be evaluated together when improving a corridor design.}

\section{Research Formulation}
\label{sec:research-formulation}

\subsection{Study Context}
\label{sec:study-context}

This study examines aircraft rerouting and surrounding traffic coordination when a localized portion of a Cooperative Area (CA) becomes unavailable at a fixed location. A CA refers to an airspace volume, such as a UAM corridor, within which operations can be cooperatively managed~\cite{FAA2023_L088}. The response occurs within the remaining available airspace and concludes for each rerouted aircraft when it rejoins an available route for continued flight.

\subsection{Research Gap and Study Focus}
\label{sec:research-gap}

Existing studies offer methods for trajectory planning, contingency management, closure verification, and network design under their respective operating assumptions~\cite{Variny2025_L015,Kim2026_L066,Wei2024_L069,Wei2024_L063}. For the localized blockage addressed in this study, there is a need to connect aircraft-level response feasibility with corridor-level evaluation and design improvement. Achieving this connection requires consistent treatment of aircraft state and energy, surrounding traffic, shared resources, and performance during normal operations. The research gap to be examined is whether existing methods support this connection under a unified set of operating assumptions. A targeted comparison will identify capabilities that can be reused and those requiring further development.

\subsection{Research Objective}
\label{sec:research-objective}

\begin{quote}
\textit{This study seeks to develop an integrated framework for emergency procedure development, performance evaluation, and corridor design improvement under localized blockages within a Cooperative Area. The framework will evaluate emergency response in conjunction with the efficiency of normal operations.}
\end{quote}

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

\subsubsection{RQ1. Emergency Procedure Development}
\label{sec:rq1}

\begin{quote}
\textit{A localized portion of a Cooperative Area at a fixed location becomes unavailable for passage. How can emergency procedures be designed to reroute and coordinate affected aircraft within the remaining available airspace while satisfying separation, aircraft performance, and energy constraints?}
\end{quote}

The work under RQ1 will develop emergency procedures and demonstrate safe and feasible flight for each aircraft represented in a defined case study. The assessment will consider each aircraft's current flight state and remaining energy, as well as surrounding traffic subject to coordination or separation requirements. Efficiency will not serve as an optimization objective in RQ1. Any conclusions regarding feasibility will apply only to the specific conditions and assumptions examined.

\subsubsection{RQ2. Performance Evaluation}
\label{sec:rq2}

\begin{quote}
\textit{How can emergency procedures and corridor designs be evaluated to assess their effects on aircraft and network performance under localized blockages?}
\end{quote}

The research conducted under RQ2 will develop an evaluation framework that links procedure and corridor design decisions to aircraft and network-level outcomes. This framework will specify operating conditions, comparison scenarios, evaluation periods, and metrics required for outcome interpretation. The assessment will address constraint satisfaction, response completion, and impacts on adjacent traffic. Normal operations will serve as a baseline for design comparisons.

\subsubsection{RQ3. Corridor Design Optimization}
\label{sec:rq3}

\begin{quote}
\textit{How can spatial corridor design and operational procedures be jointly optimized to enhance emergency response while preserving normal operational efficiency within a Cooperative Area?}
\end{quote}

The research under RQ3 will utilize the evaluation framework from RQ2 to jointly analyze spatial configurations and operational rules. Candidate designs will be evaluated for both routine operations and scenarios involving localized blockages. Improvements in emergency response will be assessed in conjunction with any impacts on normal operational efficiency. Each candidate must meet the separation, aircraft performance, and energy requirements established during procedure development. Criteria for acceptable normal performance will be specified prior to optimization.
